Abstract
Magnetic interactions between metallic granular particles can lead to magnetic cohesion, influencing the flow character­
istics of granular media. This magnetic cohesion has been studied in the context of Magneto-Rheological Fluids (MRFs) 
for their unique flow properties and their use across multiple industries. In Planetary Science, magnetic cohesion can 
influence regolith behavior on metallic asteroids with remnant magnetic fields. The upcoming NASA Psyche mission 
will study the metallic asteroid 16-Psyche, which is expected to have a surface magnetic field. Modeling and simulating 
the effect of magnetic cohesion on granular media is crucial for accurately simulating the behavior of magnetic granular 
materials in both terrestrial and planetary applications. We introduce an improved magnetic force model in LIGGGHTS, 
an open-source discrete element modeling software, to calculate magnetic forces between paramagnetic grains. The model 
is based on the Mutual Dipole Method and the Inclusion Model, extensions of the Fixed Dipole Method. We validate the 
model using 1-D unit tests and compare the results of avalanche simulations of paramagnetic regolith with experimental 
data. This work contributes to understanding the role of magnetic cohesion in small body surface processes and provides 
a tool for future studies of magnetic granular materials in DEM.

1.1  Planetary science motivation
Due to surface gravities up to 1 million times smaller than 
that on Earth, surface grains on traditional (non-metallic) 
asteroids are significantly affected by van der Waals cohe­
sion [13]. Multiple studies considered the role of cohesion 
in simulations related to NASA’s successful Double Aster­
oid Redirection Test (DART) Mission. DART impacted 
the smaller satellite object Dimorphos in the binary pair of 
65803 Didymos and Dimorphos to demonstrate the poten­
tial to change an asteroid’s trajectory for planetary defense. 
Zhang et al. [14] studied the role of cohesion in the bulk 
structural stability to maintain the fast rotational spin of the 
primary asteroid Didymos. Raducan et al. [15] evaluated 
different values for momentum change for the impact on the 
satellite Dimorphos to show that Dimorphos must have a 
weaker cohesive strength than the larger parent Didymos. 
The OSIRIS-REx mission experienced unexpected behav­
ior due to near-zero cohesion [16] during the sample collec­
tion on asteroid 101955 Bennu. Sánchez and Scheeres [17] 
looked at the cohesive properties of different fine powders 
in the context of rubble-pile asteroids. These studies demon­
strate the need to model and simulate cohesion for asteroid 
regolith research.
NASA’s Psyche spacecraft, launched in October 2023, 
is headed to the main-belt asteroid 16-Psyche. A predomi­
nantly metallic body, 16-Psyche, is assumed to be a remnant 
core of a protoplanet [18]. A dynamo could have existed in 
the initial cooling period of the asteroid [19]. This dynamo 
would result in a magnetic field being locked in the body. 
Anand et al. [20] model possible magnetic field of 16-Psyche 
due to Solar Wind Induced Magnetization (WIM). Courville 
et al. [21] theorizes volcanic dykes on 16-Psyche with mag­
netic field signatures. Solar plasma wind interaction simula­
tions by Fatemi and Poppe [22] assume surface magnetic 
fields up to 500 nT. These various theories suggest the pos­
sibility of a local surface magnetic field on 16-Psyche. The 
spacecraft is carrying a magnetometer [23, 24] to measure 
the magnetic field from orbit.
The likely constituents of M-type asteroids are iron and 
nickel. These ferromagnetic substances will have paramag­
netic ores, resulting in a paramagnetic regolith. Kohout et al. 
[25] measured magnetic susceptibilities of multiple iron and 
stony-iron meteorites. When placed in a uniform magnetic 
field, paramagnetic grains develop a magnetic moment. 
This moment results in a magnetic force acting between the 
particles, increasing the net cohesion. Sikka et al. [7] argued 
that on metallic bodies like 16-Psyche, magnetic cohesion 
can dominate over surface gravity for sub-micron-sized 
grains, while noting that a higher magnetic field strength 
would further enhance this effect for larger particles.
Robinson et al. [26] showed different gravity-driven 
morphological features on Eros 433’s three craters: Psyche, 
Himeros, and Shoemaker. Avalanching on the Moon was 
compared to the same on Earth by Kokelaar et al. [27]. 
They showed that Lunar avalanches only involve dry-flow­
ing grains, while terrestrial avalanches involve both dry and 
fluidized grains. Sugita et al. [28] demonstrated evidence 
of mass-wasting on asteroid 162173 Ryugu using Haya­
busa2 observations. The role of volatiles in mass wasting 
on Vesta and Ceres was studied by Parekh et al. [29]. They 
concluded that dry granular flows dominate Vesta’s mass-
wasting, while Ceres shows evidence of fluidized flows. 
Tang et al. [30] studied mass-wasting on Bennu’s Bralgah 
crater using DEM simulations. All these studies have shown 
the importance of cohesion in understanding mass wasting 
and granular flows on small planetary bodies.
Based on granular mechanics, DEM simulations have 
been used to model the regolith on small bodies. Research­
ers have used DEM in multiple studies for rubble pile aster­
oids ranging from reshaping & disruption [31], avalanching 
[32], and wave transmission [33]. Maurel et al. [34] simu­
lated the Brazil-nut effect on asteroid regolith. DeMartini et 
al. [35] used a proprietary software PKDGRAV to study and 
simulate the 2029 tidal encounter of Apophis 99942 with 
Earth. Frizzell and Hartzell [36–38], simulate wave trans­
mission on regolith to study the cold spot formation around 
lunar craters. Marohnic et al. [39] looked at the effect of 
particle shape on rubble-pile dynamics with a new approach 
to model non-spherical particles. Tang et al. [30] uses the 
PKDGRAV code to study mass-wasting on the asteroid 
101955 Bennu’s Bralgah Crater. Many researchers studied 
the various aspects of the DART mission target: the Dimor­
phos and Didymos system, including the impact momentum 
enhancement factor using an integrated smoothed particle 
hydrodynamics (SPH) and DEM code [40] and the effect of 
cohesion on the structural stability of Didymos using PKD­
GRAV [14].

1.2  Magneto-rheological fluids motivation
Magneto-Rheological Fluids (MRF) are suspensions of 
micron-sized, magnetizable particles in a carrier fluid [41]. 
Without an external magnetic field, the particles are ran­
domly distributed in the fluid, and the fluid behaves like a 
Newtonian fluid. When subjected to an applied magnetic 
field, the particles become magnetized and form chain-like 
structures along the field lines, thereby altering the bulk 
rheological properties, such as viscosity. This property of 
MRFs is exploited in various applications, including damp­
ers, clutches, and prosthetics. Bica et al. [42] provide a 
comprehensive review of MRF applications in various 
industries. Understanding the behavior of MRF in vari­
ous magnetic environments is crucial for optimizing these 
applications.
Modeling and simulation of MRFs is crucial for predict­
ing their behavior under various conditions. There are two 
primary approaches to modeling MRFs: continuum models 
and discrete models. Ghaffari et al. [43] provide a review of 
both the modeling and simulation techniques used for MRFs. 
Continuum models treat the system as a continuous medium 
modeled with rheological equations that describe fluid flow, 
magnetization, structural formation, and stress–strain rela­
tionships. These models are computationally faster but are 
limited by the rheological equation used and cannot capture 
particle-level interactions for every application. Finding an 
accurate rheological equation for MRFs is an ongoing area 
of research. Discrete models, on the other hand, simulate 
the behavior of magnetized particles independently of the 
carrier fluid, enabling a more detailed understanding of par­
ticle interactions and dynamics. While these models can be 
computationally expensive, they provide valuable insights 
into the microstructural behavior of MRFs. Modeling inter-
particle magnetic forces is a key research area for discrete 
models. Various methods have been proposed to model 
magnetic interactions between particles in discrete models.
Han et al. [4] reviewed different magnetic force mod­
els used in DEM simulations of MRFs. Most commonly, 
dipolar models like the Fixed Dipole Method (FDM) and 
the Mutual Dipole Method (MDM) are employed due to 
their simplicity and computational efficiency. However, 
these models have limitations in accuracy, especially when 
particles are in close proximity. Keaveny and Maxey [3] 
proposed the Inclusion Model, which improves upon dipo­
lar models, but Han et al. [4] & Ghaffari et al. [43] note 
that it is highly computationally expensive and therefore 
not widely adopted. We introduce a critical magnetic time 
step that allows the Inclusion Model to be run at a larger 
time step than the Hertzian collision time, making it more 
computationally feasible for DEM simulations. Our model 
is implemented in LIGGGHTS, an open-source DEM soft­
ware, and validated against experiments. This work is the 
first experimentally validated Inclusion model for magnetic 
interactions in DEM simulations.

1.4  Paper overview
This paper presents the implementation of the modified 
Inclusion model for magnetic interactions of spherical 
particles, as proposed by Keaveny and Maxey [3], imple­
mented in the open-source DEM software LIGGGHTS [47] 
and validated with experimental results from Sunday et al. 
[8]. Section 2 reviews the magnetostatics equations and the 
theoretical background for magnetic force models for gran­
ular particles.
Section 3 describes the implementation of the magnetic 
force model in the open-source DEM software LIGGGHTS. 
We detail the numerical methods, including the Mutual 
Dipole Method (MDM) and the Inclusion Model, and dis­
cuss computational considerations for efficient simulation. 
We also introduce the critical magnetic time step, which 
allows the magnetic force model to be run at a larger time 
step than the Hertzian collision time.
Section 4 presents validation of the implemented models 
against analytical and published results for simple particle 
systems, demonstrating the accuracy and limitations of each 
approach.
Section  5 applies the model to simulate avalanching 
of metallic grains under varying magnetic field strengths, 
comparing simulation outcomes to experimental results. 
We replicate flow regimes along with trends for angle of 
repose, surface roughness, and root mean square (RMS) 
slope, demonstrating the model’s effectiveness in capturing 
magnetic cohesion effects.
Section  7 concludes with a summary of contributions, 
limitations, and directions for future work.

3.2  MDM implementation
The MDM method described in Sect. 2.1 requires a solu­
tion to 3N × 3N linear system. For large systems with 
≈1000s of particles, even optimized C++ libraries like 
eigen require a lot of computational resources. One pos­
sible solution is to limit the dipole influence of each particle 
(eq. 6) as it directly scales with the cube of the distance from 
the particle (r3). Fazekas et al. [10] employ a similar tech­
nique for force calculation using the FDM by keeping the 
magnetic force cutoff at ≈6.25D where D is the particle 
diameter. Han et al. [4] show that for an infinite chain of 
particles, a force cutoff of ≈6a gives less than 5 % relative 
error. While force cutoff is a possible solution for the FDM 
model and dispersed/special systems, we observed that the 
aggregate of all small interactions from far-field particles

LIGGGHTS uses neighbor lists to track particle pairs and

and their neighbors. While this setup is both memory and 
computationally efficient for other DEM force models, our 
magnetic model requires all particle pairs to be considered. 
We modify the neighbor parameters for our simulations to 
generate neighbor lists that include all particle pairs.
To ensure all particle pairs are considered, we need the 
neighbor cutoff distance, known as neighbor skin dis­
tance in LIGGGHTS, to encompass the whole simulation 
domain. We refer to a neighbor list with all particle pairs 
as a complete neighbor list. This larger skin distance has 
some drawbacks. Each processor has its own neighbor list 
for each local particle. If neighbors for a particle are local to 
another processor, information pertaining to those particles 
is shared between the processors. With a complete neighbor 
list, there is a significant communication overhead. There­
fore, it is essential to optimize the number of processors 
used.

bulk susceptibility depends on the shape and size of the 
material sample. Sunday et al. [8] mentions the bulk effective 
susceptibility of the coarse alloy grains to be 3.59 ± 0.12. 
However, the material susceptibility is mentioned to range 
between 13 and 100. Sunday et al. [8] observed that the 
steel particles demonstrated plastic flow behavior starting at 
25 mT field strength. We varied the material susceptibility 
between 3.6 and 40.0 at a field strength of 25 mT. With the 
Inclusion model, we could reproduce the flow behavior with 
a material susceptibility of 20.0.

5.5  Data analysis
To analyze the simulation results, we examine three dif­
ferent granular flow parameters: Flow Behavior, Angle of 
Repose, and Surface Roughness [8]. Surface roughness is 
determined using the root-mean-squared (RMS) height 
and the RMS slope. Python, with the NumPy, SciPy, and 
Pandas libraries, is used for data analysis, and Ovito Pro 
[57] is used for visualization. We visually analyze the flow 
behavior to assess whether our model can recreate the three 
flow regimes: granular, correlated, and plastic. The angle of 
repose and surface roughness were analyzed by comparing 
the top particles in the pre-avalanche and the post-avalanche 
state. To find the top particles, we divide the simulation box 
into a grid along the x axis with cells of particle radius size 
(a × w × h, where a is the particle radius, w is the simula­
tion width, and h is the height of the simulation box). We 
then find the highest particle in each grid cell and look at 
any particle with vertical displacement less than half the 
particle radius ∆y < ϵ = a/2 to find the list of stationary 
particles that haven’t moved between the two frames.
We look at two different values for the angle of repose 
(θ) and surface roughness (RMS height ξ and RMS slope 
θrms). First, as in Sunday et al. [8], we collapse the y−
dimension (width) of the simulation and find general values. 
Second, we also analyze the variation of these parameters in 
the y−axis (width) by calculating them for different slices 
(Fig. 11). We divide the simulation width into five equal 
y-slices and compute the angle of repose, RMS height, and 
RMS slope for each y-slice. Therefore, we are able to obtain 
multiple unique values for each of our three data analysis 
parameters: 3 or 4 from different seeds and 5 within each 
seed from the y−slicing.
However, in some cases with higher magnetic cohesion, not 
all particles come to a complete stop. Some particles fall 
into a steady state around local magnetic fields, oscillating 
around a local minimum.
Once the assembly is settled, we use this simulation 
frame as our pre-avalanche state. Then, an avalanche is ini­
tiated by deleting (using LIGGGHTS unfix command) the 
middle partition wall. The particles are then allowed to fall 
freely under the influence of gravity. We run each simula­
tion for at least 1.5 s more, after which we start checking for 
the steady state. Once a steady state is reached, we treat the 
last frame as the post-avalanche state.

6  Limitations
A few limitations of the current implementation in 
LIGGGHTS are worth noting. LIGGGHTS processor map­
ping in large simulations leads to a non-uniform distribution 
of particles across processors. This results in an inefficient 
use of processor resources and longer computation times. 
Changing the LIGGGHTS processor distribution or using 
a different DEM software like LAMMPS (balance com­
mand) helps achieve a more uniform distribution and there­
fore faster computation times. This could also enable us to 
run the Inclusion model with larger multipoles to achieve 
higher magnetic susceptibilities.
LIGGGHTS calculates forces between particle neighbor 
pairs only. The current implementation circumvents this 
limitation by using a large neighbor skin distance to include 
all particles as neighbors. This leads to issues with neighbor 
list construction requiring more computational resources. 
The assignment of particles to processors is carried out 
whenever a neighbor list is constructed, which can lead to a 
significant slowdown in the simulation. Future work could 
include optimizing the neighbor list construction to reduce 
the computational overhead.
A very quirky behavior was observed while using mul­
tiple run commands in LIGGGHTS. The run command in 
LIGGGHTS is used to advance the simulation by a specified 
number of time-steps. Each run command starts with a pre-
construction of neighbor lists and forces. We observed an 
influx of energy into the system whenever a subsequent run 
command was used after the first one. This was not observed 
when using a single run command for the entire simula­
tion. We suspect this is due to the large neighbor skin dis­
tance that includes all particles as neighbors. We tried using 
the run pre no command, which does not reconstruct 
the forces, but it did not work as expected. This issue can 
be avoided by using a single run command for the entire 
simulation. But this is not always feasible for longer simu­
lations, for example, removing the middle wall requires at 
least two run commands. We therefore experimented with 
the run command and found that the influx of energy is dis­
sipated within 1-10 dump time-steps, which is set to 1 ms in 
our simulations. Therefore, we can safely ignore the influx 
of energy if we use run commands with time steps larger 
than an order of magnitude of 10 ms.
Along with the limitations in LIGGGHTS, the Inclusion 
model itself has some limitations. The model is based on 
the assumption that all particles are identical (same mag­
netic susceptibility and size) and spherical. Therefore, this 
model cannot simulate a more complex particle shape or 
distribution. While the particle shape effects can be mod­
eled by varying friction parameters, different magnetic sus­
ceptibilities cannot be modeled. We can, however, use the 
percentage increase remains the same. Therefore, the mod­
eling matches the experimental results.
We do not include the RMS slope data from the 5 slices, 
as the RMS slope is sensitive to the number of particles con­
sidered, and the slices significantly reduce the number of 
particles. The RMS slope values from the slices were all 
over 40◦. Figure 15 shows the top particles considered in 
each slice for the no field case. Each slice has a different 
number of top particles considered, resulting in higher RMS 
slope values compared to the collapsed width case.
We performed MDM simulations for a single seed to 
compare the results to the Inclusion model. Figure 13 shows 
the final frames for the MDM model with different field 
strengths, while Fig. 14 shows the angle of repose and sur­
face roughness trends with increasing field strength. The 
figures show that the MDM model cannot reproduce the 
three flow regimes. We could not reach the plastic regime 
even at the highest field strength of 25 mT. Moreover, the 
MDM model continued to exhibit a granular flow regime 
at the fields, where the Inclusion model showed a corre­
lated and plastic regime (15-25 mT). We observed that, at 
higher fields, even a single moving particle can disrupt the 
entire pile. This behavior led to longer settling times (> 5s) 
while all Inclusion simulations reached steady state within 
2 s. Without the correlated and plastic regimes, the surface 
roughness parameters did not show a significant increase 
with increasing field strength. The angle of repose instead 
showed a slight decrease, 20.34◦± 0.81◦ (no field) to 
17.96◦± 1.33◦ (25 mT).
We were not able to perform Inclusion simulations with 
6.35 mm particles and higher susceptibilities due to com­
putational limitations. Therefore, simulations with a single 
seed were performed with a magnetic susceptibility of 3.6. 
At the same time, we varied the field strength from 5 mT to 
100 mT to achieve similar magnetic cohesion as the 8.73 
mm particles with a susceptibility of 20.0 at 25 mT. We were 
able to reproduce the three flow regimes with the Inclusion 
model for 6.35 mm particles. We observed a granular flow 
regime at 5 mT, a correlated flow regime at 30-65 mT, and 
a plastic flow regime at 80-100 mT. Figure 16 shows the 
final frames for the Inclusion model with different field 
strengths, while Fig. 17 shows the angle of repose and sur­
face roughness trends with increasing field strength. The 
slight increase in angle of repose from 18.81◦± 0.17◦ (no 
field) to 20.50◦± 0.20◦ (30 mT) is attributed to the lower 
susceptibility. While 8.73 mm simulations validated the 
model against experimental results, the 6.35 mm simula­
tions showcase the three flow regimes for another particle 
size and susceptibility pair, providing further confidence 
that the Inclusion model accurately models the magnetic 
interactions.

7  Conclusions
We have presented an accurate and experimentally validated 
magnetic force model for discrete element method (DEM) 
simulations of metallic grains. The model is based on the 
Inclusion model of Keaveny and Maxey [3] and is imple­
mented in the open-source software LIGGGHTS. We also 
defined a new critical magnetic time to calculate the mag­
netic force at a lower frequency than the DEM time step. 
This new model is more accurate than the readily used 
Mutual Dipole Model (MDM), as shown in our compari­
sons. We validated the model against simple 1-D test cases 
and then used it to simulate avalanching of metallic grains. 
Earlier studies by Fazekas et al. [58] and Taylor et al. [11] 
using dipolar models did not reproduce the plastic regime. 
Our model replicated the bulk behavior of metallic grains 
in avalanching experiments by Sunday et al. [8], showcas­
ing three distinct flow regimes: granular, correlated, and 
plastic. While earlier computational studies did showcase 
an increase in the angle of repose with an external magnetic 
field, they did not compare the trends with experimental 
results. We demonstrated a quantitative increase in the angle 
of repose and surface roughness parameters with increasing 
magnetic field strength, in agreement with the experimental 
data.
7.1  Future work
The current Angle of Repose (AoR) and surface roughness 
analysis were primarily done in a 2D manner by collaps­
ing the y−dimension. We sliced the simulation width into 
five equal slices to analyze the variation of these parameters 
in the y−axis. Future work should include a more detailed 
3D analysis of the angle of repose and surface roughness 
parameters. The current analysis is limited to comparing the 
pre-avalanche and post-avalanche states. An advantage of 
simulations over experimental work is the ability to track 
the trajectory of each particle, along with the force data. 
We can use this information to analyze avalanche dynam­
ics in more detail by looking at force chains and particle 
interactions.
The new magnetic force model enables us to simulate 
more accurate and complex scenarios for magnetic cohesion 
in granular materials. We can use the Inclusion model to 
simulate the effect of particle shape on avalanching behav­
ior by varying the rolling friction parameters. We can also 
model the effect of gravity on the change in angle of repose 
and surface roughness. These simulations would enable
