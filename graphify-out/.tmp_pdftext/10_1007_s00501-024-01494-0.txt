A Discrete Element Method Calibration Approach for Large
Ore-ﬂow Applications
Lukas Alexander Koch
Chair of Mining Engineering and Mineral Economics, Montanuniversität Leoben, Leoben, Austria
Received June 18, 2024; accepted June 21, 2024; published online August 15, 2024
Abstract: The major constraint for large discrete element
method (DEM) simulations is the high demand for com-
putation power.
To keep the model in a feasible range
of simulation time and to conduct the numerical calcula-
tions efﬁciently, the implemented material can be simpli-
ﬁed and upscaled.
After this, a calibration of the mate-
rial is inevitable and sets the link between the simulation
model and reality.
Therefore, an alternative calibration
approach is established in this contribution to mimic the
ﬂow behaviour of the original material with multi-spherical
DEM particles. The calibration approach utilises ﬂow zone
dimensions, which were observed in block-caving opera-
tions. Additionally, apassivedraw-point isused to calibrate
the angle of repose. Applying the calibration approach for
Kiruna iron ore showed that, with the help of the calibration
approach, the material in the simulation could successfully
reproduce the targeted ﬂow zone and angle of repose.
Keywords: Discrete Element Method, Calibration, Cave
mining, Ore-ﬂow

1. Introduction
There are several challenges when investigating large mod-
elscontaining millionsof particleswith the discrete element
method (DEM). One of the most important challenges is
the high demand for computing capacity. First of all, soft-
ware with a large parallelisation capability is thus neces-
sary, preferably with the possibility to run the simulation
on a graphics card (GPU). In this contribution the software
EDEM by Altair [1] was used. This contribution further con-
centrates on two points: simplifying the material in the
model to reduce the high demand for computing power and
subsequently calibrating the simpliﬁed material to yield re-
alistic results as if it was the original material.
A DEM calibration can compensate to some extent for
simpliﬁcation measures undertaken beforehand. The cali-
bration sometimes represents the largest part and a major
difﬁculty in large-scale DEM simulations [2, 3]. For a DEM
simulation, the material macro propertiesare distinguished
from the DEM micro parameters [4]. The macro properties
describe the macroscopic behaviour of the bulk material,
e.g. the angle of repose or the ﬂow zone dimensions in
a cave mine.
The DEM micro parameters, on the other
hand, are the input parameters for the software which lead

1.2 Approach
The idea of the calibration approach is to generate a virtual
material that possesses the same ﬂow behaviour as the
real physical one. Therefore, the physical one as well as its
known macroscopic features need to be determined in the
beginning.
To decrease the computational demand and
to enable a suitable DEM simulation, the virtual material
usually needs to be simpliﬁed by different measures. The
contact parameters, which are mainly the friction values
(referring to a common Hertz-Mindling contact model), are
then adapted in the calibration process, and the calibration
simulation is repeated until the macroscopic behaviour cor-
responds to the behaviour of the physical material. When
the virtual material behaviour ﬁts, the calibrated parameter
set is found, and the calibration is ﬁnished. If simpliﬁcation
measures are used too extensively, a successful calibration
may not be possible.
Figure 1 summarises the systematic of this contribu-
tion’s calibration approach. First, the basic properties of
the original material that is expected to be encountered in
the mine need to be deﬁned (particle shape, particle size
distribution, density etc.). Then the target values for the
calibration are determined, which are the angle of repose
and the draw-zone width, as explained later in more detail.
To keep the number of required calibration simulations as
small as possible, both calibration values are implemented
in asinglecalibration model. The material isthen simpliﬁed
to enable the calculation of the model. Subsequently, the
simpliﬁed material is inserted with a predeﬁned bulk den-
sity into the calibration model. A sensitivity study is run
by iteratively adapting the friction parameters and running
the simulation until the target values of the calibration are
met. To keep the number of simulations low, both chosen
target values are evaluated in a single model. If the results
correspond to the target values, the calibration is ﬁnished.
2. Calibration Approach
The presented calibration model is based on experiences
on movement zones above a draw-point, made by Laub-
scher [17] in BC operations. Further on, the angle of repose
at thedraw-point iscompared to arepresentativeone found
in the mine. The model, seen in Fig. 2a, consists of a stope
of 25× 30× 40m with broken material. On the bottom of the
model, there is a draw-bell with a 45° inclination and two
attached draw-points. The vertical distance from the ﬂoor
of the extraction point to the bottom of the stope above is
10m. Material is extracted from one—active—draw-point,
which evokes a movement zone in the material pile above.
From the second—passive—draw-point material ﬂows out
and stabilises at a certain angle of repose. The draw-points
have a quadratic cross-section of 5× 5m and a straight roof.
These dimensions were taken from typical BC layouts [18,
19].
To extract material from the active draw-point, the ma-
terial is removed after the outﬂow through a vertical ex-
tension below the draw-point. Consequently, the overly-
ing material begins to ﬂow towards the draw-point, and
a movement zone evolves.
Material is allowed to ﬂow
out until the movement zone breaks through the top of
the model, which is ﬁlled to a height of 40m (see Fig. 2b).
After this breakthrough point, the zone above the moving
particles is destabilised. The simulations were then anal-
ysed and compared. Depending on the parameter set, the

2.1 Determination of a Representative Material
The ﬁrst step in replicating material in a DEM model is to
determine the representative physical material in nature.
Therefore, the fragmentation size and the particle shape
are estimated. The presented approach is utilised in ore-
ﬂow investigations for the Kiruna mine, researching a novel
mining method called Raise-Caving [20]. The particle size
distribution (PSD) is taken from available literature sources
[21] stating the Kiruna SLC situation with an x50 value of
110mm (see Fig. 3). Future investigations should also tar-
get other fragmentation groups, which include coarse and
homogeneous, coarse and inhomogeneous groups, and
the inﬂuence of water and cohesion.
Regarding the particle shape, pictures from the material
at SLC draw-points in the Kiruna mine (see Fig. 4) gave the
impression of a rather angular material shape. This infor-
mation was seen to be sufﬁcient for modeling, as the over-
all material behaviour calibration compensates for a certain
lack of particle-shape detail.

2.2 Target Values for Calibration
The basis for the calibration is the experience made in
many BC operations by Laubscher [17]. Laubscher showed
that the size of extraction zones above a draw-point de-
pended on the PSD and the dimensions of the draw-point
(see Fig. 5). In this table, the loading width indicates the
width of the draw-point. The rock mass class deﬁnes the
size of the caved rock mass. Wide ﬂow zones above the
draw-points are achieved when the material is very coarse.
The ﬂow zone gets slimmer with ﬁner material.
The fragmentation data seen in Fig. 3 wascombined with
a draw-point width of 5m, which resulted in a draw-zone
diameter of approximately 10m. This was consequently
set as the target value for the calibration. For the angle
of repose, Määttä et al. [22] showed for 3D-laser-scanned
Kiruna draw-points that a normal and stable gravity ﬂow
took place when the angle of repose varied between 30–35°.
Beakawi [23] analysed that, for coarse gravel material, val-
ues of 25–45° could be possible, depending on the content
of ﬁnes. It was seen in literature that the angle of repose
increases for ﬁner material [23, 24]. As a combination of
this information, a range of 33–37° was set as a target for
the angle of repose of the investigated ﬁne material group.
2.3 Simpliﬁcation Measures on Particle
Properties
2.3.1 PSD and Particle Shape Simpliﬁcation
The simpliﬁcation measures are applied to the determined
material. These need to be implemented within their lim-
its to yield realistic results. The ﬁrst simpliﬁcation mea-
sures are to neglect ﬁnes and simplify the particle shape.
An issue with implementing ﬁnes is the excessive calcu-
lation demand as these contain billions of relatively small
(and, respectively, computationally intensive) particles. In
state-of-the-art simulations, ﬁnes are commonly neglected.
The lack of this part of fragmentation needs to be compen-
sated with a proper calibration. Additionally, it was seen in
the course of this contribution that the implementation of
large boulders—although reducing the number of particles
in a simulation—affected the computational speed nega-
tively, as the time step was reduced to maintain the particle
overlaps in a relatively small range. Boulders in Kiruna are
deﬁned as material blocks larger than 0.7m. However, the
fraction of particles larger than 0.5m is already very small
and was therefore neglected in the basic models. The im-
plemented PSD consequently followed the curve of Fig. 3
by only considering the sizes 50–500mm. To generate this
PSD in the DEM model, four different particle size groups
were used—50, 150, 300, and 500mm.
The particle shape has a signiﬁcant effect on the bulk ma-
terial behaviour; however, literature references [3, 4] show
that an exact replication of the particle shape in DEM simu-
lation isoften not necessary. Complex particlescan berepli-
cated in DEM simulations by simpliﬁed particle shapes. For
example, multiple spheres can be consolidated into multi-
sphere particles. They are inseparably ﬁxed together and

2.3.2 Shear Modulus, Coefﬁcient of Restitution,
and Upscaling Factor
A further point to speed up the simulation is to reduce the
shear modulus in the model compared to reality. This can
yield massively faster results. If applied within its limits,
it does not change the quality of the result for many appli-
cations [25, 26]. The shear modulus reduction was tested
by Brandner and Koch [27]. As a consequence, the shear
modulus was reduced in this contribution from originally
34 to 5GPa. Considering the value of the coefﬁcient of resti-
tution (COR), it was found by different authors [26, 28] that
DEM simulations showed an insensitivity to the variation
of the COR. Its exact determination can additionally only
be done individually for every particle with drop tests. This
wasseen to be impractical for a calibration process, which is
whyliteratureresearch on thisparameter wasdone. Barrios
et al. [29] investigated similar material properties to crude
iron ore and used the same software as in this contribution
(EDEM). Therefore, the found COR value of 0.51 served as
the guiding value, with a value of 0.5 implemented in this
work.
The calibration model with 25× 30× 40m dimensions
still contains over a hundred million of the simpliﬁed parti-
cles, which is not feasible to compute with contemporary
workstations. A solution to reduce the number of particles
is to apply particle upscaling. The idea is to replace the
particles by larger ones.
Up to a certain point, speciﬁ-
cally depending on the application, this was seen not to
affect the results adversely, especially when a calibration
is conducted subsequently. For large stope applications,
this was tested by Brandner and Koch [27] and seen to

of the broken ore in the stope due to blasting and com-

To set up the simulation model, the material is dropped
into the stope from the top. It was seen that the predeﬁned
friction values during the ﬁlling process already inﬂuenced
the bulk density of the model. Higher friction led to a lower
bulk density and vice versa. To ﬁnd adequate friction values
for the ﬁlling process, a parameter study was done. A box
was ﬁlled several times with different friction values. The
static and rolling friction values were varied during each
ﬁlling process, from 0.1, to 0.5, and 0.9. Thisisdone for both

the rolling friction should be set lower than the staticfriction

be achieved by four different parameter combinations. To
compensate for any larger voids across the entire stope, the
lower value of the range was chosen. Therefore the used
density was 3111kg/m3, achieved by the parameter set of
0.5 (static friction) and 0.1 (rolling friction).

3. Conclusion, Summary, and Outlook
The contribution showed a successful way to calibrate ma-
terial in large discrete element method (DEM) models for
cave mining. This was based on full-scale mine data, which
included the ﬂow width within a stope and the angle of
repose of a passive draw-point.
The model consists of
a large stope with broken material, an underlying draw-
bell with a 45° inclination, and two draw-points at the bot-
tom. One draw-point is actively worked and consequently
forms a ﬂow zone of broken material in the draw-bell above
it. The other draw-point is passive, and the outﬂowing ma-
terial forms an angle of repose.
The calibration targeted the ﬂow behaviour of the iron
ore in the Kiruna mine.
Therefore characteristic proper-
ties concerning the particle shape and particle size distri-
bution were implemented in the software. Simpliﬁcation
measures were applied within the tested limits to facilitate
the computation. A parameter study was conducted to ﬁnd
suitable friction parameters for the ﬁlling process in order
